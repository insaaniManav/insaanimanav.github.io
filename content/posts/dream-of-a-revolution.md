This post took me two weeks to write and I think you'll be able to tell.

The book is a biography of J.P. Narayan, started by Bimal Prasad and finished by his daughter Sujata Prasad - both civil servants, both close enough to JP and his family. The reason I picked it up was the same reason as "India After Gandhi": trying to build some exposure to politics that doesn't come from my feed.

Now J.P. Narayan is a name that most young Indians have heard of but really aren't able to place who he was or his significance in the Independence movement.

He was not born into a wealthy or political family at all, most of his love for revolution came from his understanding and readings of Marxist literature. Started a formal education in Patna Collegiate School. After which he moved to Patna College, but soon as a part of the Non-Cooperation movement and to not support British Colonial Institutions, he moved to the USA.

In the US, he rotated between universities simply because he had to fund himself with odd jobs (picking grapes, canning fruit, washing dishes, garage mechanic work) and this is where he worked firsthand as a labourer, someone from the "working class", and this first-hand experience is what I believe led him to drift toward Marxism and later Socialism.

This is the first thing that stood out to me in this book. I had been to Anand Bhawan, Nehru's residence in Prayagraj, not too long ago.

A mansion with its own gardens, where the family hosted the entire independence movement.

JP was washing dishes in California to pay for his degree.

Two men who ended up in the same rooms arguing about the same country, arriving there from completely different places.

The rest of what stuck with me is all about timing going wrong for him.

He spent his whole life wanting what he called a "True and Ultimate Revolution," by violent or non-violent means. By the time it actually arrived in Bihar he was too old to lead it properly.

He spent decades as a committed Marxist before turning into a Gandhian socialist, which is a strange direction to travel at that stage of a life.

And then the IB announced his death on the radio before it happened. Parliament literally observed 2 minutes of silence for a very alive man. About 30 minutes later, Janata Party President Chandra Shekhar announced from the Bombay hospital that JP was still alive. JP reportedly smiled when told of the incident later and passed away months later.

Overall though to me, this book just wasn't that engaging or interesting at all. To someone who reallllly admires this man, or to someone who realllly wants to know who he was and what he stood for, maybe this will be a wonderful read but it just wasn't for me.

Lowkey put me in a reading slump but I am fully back with "The Accidental Prime Minister"

---

One thing I kept thinking about while reading: JP took about thirty years to get to Bihar.

He organised through Sarvodaya from the fifties onward, walked through villages, built a network across states, spent years on land reform before any of it turned into a movement. By the time he called for Total Revolution in 1974 he had three decades of groundwork behind him and a country that already knew who he was.

I don't know what the equivalent of that looks like now. Movements assemble in a week and dissolve in a month. Maybe that's a loss, maybe it's just what organising looks like when coordination is free and everyone can reach everyone. I genuinely don't know. But reading about the thirty years made me notice the difference.
